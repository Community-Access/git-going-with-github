[JAMIE]
So this is not just “type something in the box.” It is learning how to contribute in a way other people can use.

[PAUSE]

[ALEX]
Exactly. The goal is to leave a comment that is useful, use an @mention so the right person gets notified, and add a reaction when that fits. We are teaching the move, the reason behind it, and the evidence that shows it happened.

[JAMIE]
And the example exchange should make that feel real, not abstract.

[ALEX]
In the example exchange, your comment lands on a buddy’s issue. It says what you noticed, adds a helpful thought, and keeps the tone constructive. Then the buddy replies with a thanks or a follow-up, and the conversation keeps moving.

[JAMIE]
So a good comment is not just agreement. It is something specific the other person can act on.

[ALEX]
Right. A strong comment usually does three things: it refers to the exact problem, it adds something useful, and it stays friendly. If you can say, “I saw the same thing in this file” or “Maybe this line needs a clearer label,” you are in the right zone.

[ALEX]
Alternate approaches count too. You can ask a clarifying question, point to a line number, or suggest a next step. What matters is that the comment helps the issue move forward instead of just adding noise.

[JAMIE]
That also explains the reaction part. Sometimes a quick reaction is enough to show you saw the thread.

[ALEX]
Yes. A reaction is small, but it still signals participation. The bigger lesson is constructive peer communication: read carefully, respond clearly, and keep the exchange helpful.

[ALEX]
Start with what a GitHub issue is. An issue is a tracked conversation about work: a bug, a task, a question, or a request. It has a title, a body, comments, and activity that help everyone follow along.

[JAMIE]
So it is both a record and a conversation.

[ALEX]
Exactly. And the first step is usually finding the Issues list.

[ALEX]
You can get there from the repository page by selecting the Issues tab, or by using the direct repository Issues URL. Once there, you are looking at the list page, not a single issue yet.

[JAMIE]
What should the learner notice on that list page?

[ALEX]
The page structure matters. The list shows issue titles, open or closed state, labels, and recent activity. Reading the list is about spotting what is new, what is active, and what is already done.

[ALEX]
Each issue announcement gives you a compact summary: what the issue is about, who is involved, and whether it is still open. That is enough to decide where to click next.

[PAUSE]

[JAMIE]
How do filtering and searching fit in?

[ALEX]
Use the search and filter bar when you want to narrow the list. Helpful queries might include labels, authors, keywords, or open issues only. The filter buttons also help you switch views fast.

[JAMIE]
And open versus closed is one of the big ones.

[ALEX]
Yes. Open issues are still active. Closed issues are done or resolved. Learning to switch between them is a basic issue-management skill.

[ALEX]
The important habit is to search before you guess. If the issue already exists, you can join it instead of making a duplicate.

[PAUSE]

[JAMIE]
Now take us into reading an issue.

[ALEX]
When you open an issue, start with the title and description. Then scan the comments and activity so you understand the context. If there are labels, assignees, or milestones, those can help too.

[JAMIE]
So the learner is reading for the problem, the current status, and the people involved.

[ALEX]
Exactly. Quick navigation helps here too. Move through the page in a predictable order: title, body, comments, and then any linked activity. That makes the page less overwhelming.

[ALEX]
A good comment starts with that same habit. Read the issue carefully before replying, because the best responses refer to what was actually said.

[PAUSE]

[ALEX]
Challenge 2 is Create Your First Issue. Open your Learning Room repository, go to the Issues tab, and create a new issue with a clear title and a useful description.

[JAMIE]
So the title should say what the issue is, not just “help” or “bug.”

[ALEX]
Right. The title field should be specific. The body should explain what happened, what you expected, and any details that would help someone reproduce the problem.

[ALEX]
If you are filling out the issue form, use the prompts the form gives you. Title. Description or body. What happened. What I expected. How to reproduce. Environment. Additional context. Those fields help the reader understand the report fast.

[JAMIE]
That makes the issue easier to triage and easier to fix.

[ALEX]
And after you submit it, note the issue number or URL. That is the evidence you will use later.

And the @mention is what notifies the right person.
